# Competitor Research and Scoring

Use this guide whenever the canvas includes named competitors or current market claims.

## Build the comparator set

- Start with the target buyer and the job, not a vendor list.
- Cover meaningful choice types: direct product, substitute, status quo or manual work, internal build, service-led option, and a low- or high-end alternative when relevant.
- Prefer 2-5 comparators that reveal distinct value curves. Avoid several nearly identical vendors unless their differences matter to the decision.
- For each candidate, record whether it is plotted, represented by an archetype, or excluded. State the buyer-choice reason for that decision.
- When more than four comparators must be plotted, keep them all in the score table and split the visual into panels of four comparators plus the current offering.

## Research evidence

Use this source order:

1. First-party product, pricing, documentation, policy, and support pages
2. First-party customer stories for documented capabilities or target segments
3. Credible independent reporting, reviews, or research for buyer context and limitations
4. User-supplied interviews, win/loss notes, support data, or internal research

For each material claim, retain:

- Claim or observation
- Source link or user-provided evidence label
- Publisher and publication date when available
- Research or access date
- Factor the evidence informs
- Classification: `Observed`, `Inferred`, or `Hypothesis`

Cite claims in the document, not inside the chart. Put `Scores are strategic hypotheses; sources are documented in the report` in the chart footer.

## Separate facts from scores

- Treat documented capabilities, published prices, and stated policies as observations.
- Treat likely buyer impact, relative strength, and missing-capability conclusions as inferences unless direct buyer evidence supports them.
- Treat every 1-5 score as a strategic hypothesis. Evidence supports the hypothesis but does not turn the score into a measured fact.
- Compare the same target buyer, use case, geography, plan level, and time period across alternatives.
- Do not interpret absence from public documentation as proof that a capability does not exist.

## Assign confidence

- `High`: current direct evidence or repeated buyer evidence clearly supports the relative position.
- `Medium`: multiple indirect signals support the position, but an important assumption remains.
- `Low`: the score is provisional, based on a single weak source, inferred from missing information, stale, or disputed.

Use exactly `High`, `Medium`, or `Low`; do not create hybrid labels such as `Medium-high`. Use an overall confidence in each dossier. Surface factor-level confidence only for pivotal or low-confidence scores.

## Write compact dossiers

Use this structure for every plotted comparator:

### Comparator name

- **Buyer alternative:** What the target buyer would use it to accomplish.
- **Category:** Direct competitor, substitute, status quo, internal build, service, or other archetype.
- **Why included:** The distinct value curve or buyer choice it represents.
- **Defining strengths:** Two or three evidence-backed strengths relevant to the factors.
- **Defining weaknesses:** One or two evidence-backed limitations or explicit hypotheses.
- **Pivotal score rationale:** Only the scores that drive the chart's interpretation.
- **Evidence:** Inline citations with source or research dates.
- **Confidence:** High, Medium, or Low, with a short reason.

After the dossiers, add a compact `Supporting alternatives` note for named but unplotted choices. Explain the representative comparator or exclusion reason.

## Guardrails

- Never invent competitor behavior, customer sentiment, price, packaging, financial impact, market share, dates, or source confidence.
- Do not copy vendor marketing claims as neutral truth; attribute them and interpret them separately.
- Do not use one factor definition for the current offering and a different definition for competitors.
- Do not reward feature count when the factor is buyer outcome, effort, risk, or cost.
- Call out evidence gaps that could reverse a pivotal score or strategic conclusion.
